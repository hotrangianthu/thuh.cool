## Essay 1: Why Public Policy, Why This Work
*Prompt: Why pursue advanced policy work now, and how does it align with your professional and personal goals?*
*Target: ~240 words*

---

I have spent a decade building financial technology products for Southeast Asia's largest super-app. I watched digital payment and credit products reach tens of millions of users, and repeatedly fail rural users. Not because rural Vietnamese lack ambition or capability, but because the policy infrastructure required to make digital finance viable in their context remains incomplete.

This is not only a product problem. It is a policy architecture problem. Solving it requires deeper training in policy analysis, institutional design, and implementation strategy than private sector experience alone can provide.

My work is now focused on applied policy questions in Vietnam: rural digital inclusion, province-differentiated investment frameworks, and governance design for the digital economy. These are practical questions with high public impact and measurable outcomes.

I am not changing career tracks. I am adding policy craft to an existing execution background so the work can matter at national scale. This portfolio is part of that commitment: a public body of analysis, hypotheses, and prototypes that can be challenged, improved, and built on over time.

## Essay 2: The Policy Problem I Want to Solve
*Prompt: Describe a significant policy problem in Vietnam that you are passionate about addressing. What do you see as the root cause, and what approaches might you recommend?*
*Target: ~370 words*

---

Sixty-two million rural Vietnamese participate in the digital economy at the margins of a system designed without them in mind. Mobile banking ownership is 31 percent in rural areas versus 78 percent in cities. Access to mobile credit is 11 percent versus 45 percent. These are structural gaps, not temporary lags.

Vietnam's 2025-2035 development strategy is ambitious, targeting 8 percent annual growth through digital transformation. Yet it contains no integrated framework for rural digital inclusion. The Ministry of Information and Communications manages connectivity. The State Bank manages financial inclusion. The Ministry of Agriculture manages rural productivity. None is accountable for a shared rural digital outcome.

The root cause is institutional, not technical. Connectivity is improving. Devices are affordable. What is missing is coordinated policy design: who owns rural digital financial access, what regulatory model enables agent banking at commune level, and how provinces are incentivized to implement inclusion mandates with limited administrative capacity.

My approach centers on three pillars. First, Access: mandated agent banking rollout tied to commune thresholds with interoperability standards. Second, Literacy: a rural digital skills program through commune learning centers focused on productive use cases. Third, Productivity: incentives for rural digital commerce, including e-payment mandates for public services and agricultural e-market infrastructure.

These pillars are interdependent. Access without literacy yields low adoption. Literacy without productive use cases atrophies. A national framework must design all three together and assign institutional accountability clearly.

This is the paper I am already writing. It is also the career I am building.

## Essay 3: Overcoming a Thinking Barrier
*Prompt: Describe a time when you had to challenge your own assumptions or overcome a significant intellectual or professional barrier. What did you learn?*
*Target: ~310 words*

---

For the first seven years of my career at Grab, I believed good products were sufficient. Build the right experience, optimize conversion, reduce acquisition cost, and access would follow. It was disciplined and data-driven thinking.

It was also wrong in an important way.

In 2022, I led a market expansion analysis for a rural fintech product in Vietnam's Mekong Delta. Unit economics looked feasible. Infrastructure existed. Partnerships were available. The product worked. Adoption still flatlined at rates far below urban benchmarks, not due to awareness gaps, but because users lacked trust in digital finance, practical digital literacy, and local merchant acceptance.

Those were not product problems. They were policy and institutional problems: weak literacy infrastructure, insufficient agent networks for trust-building, and regulatory ambiguity that constrained product design for rural users.

I had to challenge an assumption I had never examined closely: that private innovation could substitute for policy architecture. It cannot. Even excellent products cannot close inclusion gaps created by unclear regulation, fragmented accountability, and underinvestment in human infrastructure.

That lesson changed how I work. It pushed me into welfare economics, institutional analysis, and policy design as practical tools, not abstract theory. It is why this portfolio exists and why I now build research in public.
